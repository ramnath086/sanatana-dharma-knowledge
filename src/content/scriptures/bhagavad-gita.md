The Bhagavad Gītā (c. 2nd c. BCE–2nd c. CE, 700 verses) is the philosophical core of the Mahābhārata (Bhīṣma Parva, chap. 23–40) and the dialogue between Kṛṣṇa and the reluctant Arjuna on the field of Kurukṣetra. It synthesizes the three yogas — karma (selfless action), jñāna (knowledge), bhakti (devotion) — and affirms the four Puruṣārthas. Its doctrines (līlā, mokṣa, the gunas, the supreme person of the Gītā, nis-kāma karma) shaped the Vedānta, the bhakti movement, and modern Indian political and religious thought (Gandhi, Tilak, Aurobindo). It is the most translated and most commented Hindu text.
